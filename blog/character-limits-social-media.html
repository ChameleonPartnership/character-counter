Character Limits Across Social Media

Published 13 July 2026. Updated 1 September 2026. 6 min read.

Tool Cheat sheet Blog About Contact

Social platforms punish vague writing quickly. A post that looks tidy in a document can be clipped after one line, buried behind a see more link, or rejected because a caption, title or bio field has a hard limit. Good copy teams treat limits as part of the brief, not as a final clean-up chore.

Use the privacy-first character counter while you edit. Your text stays in the browser, and the limit bars give a quick check before you paste copy into a publishing tool.

The limits that matter most

X still uses 280 characters as the familiar public-post limit for standard posts. That number is enough for a claim, one proof point and a short call to action, but not enough for a full argument. For professional publishing, draft the post in two layers: first the sentence that must survive on its own, then the supporting phrase that earns its place.

LinkedIn gives far more room, with ordinary posts often planned around a 3,000-character ceiling. The practical problem is not the ceiling. It is the early preview. If the first two lines do not state the point, the rest of the post asks too much of a busy reader. Start with the result, decision or tension before adding context.

Instagram captions and TikTok captions both allow long copy, commonly planned around 2,200 characters. Long does not mean useful. Captions work best when they support the visual: a sharp hook, a few details, a short instruction, then selected hashtags. Count hashtags as copy because the reader sees them and the platform counts them.

Write for the preview, not only the maximum

The maximum limit is the last line of defence. The preview is where performance is won or lost. A LinkedIn update with 2,400 characters can still fail if the first 140 characters are slow. An Instagram caption can fit perfectly and still hide the discount code after the fold. A YouTube title can use all 100 characters and still bury the searchable phrase.

Put the clearest noun early. Instead of "A few notes from the work we have been doing on checkout errors", write "Checkout errors dropped 18% after three copy changes". The second version spends 55 characters and gives the reader a reason to continue. It also works as a headline, post opener and internal case-study label.

When a post needs approval, send reviewers the count with the copy. A line such as "LinkedIn opener: 126 characters. Full post: 1,840 characters" makes feedback more precise. It stops the conversation drifting into taste and keeps the team focused on whether the message fits the channel.

Platform notes for copy teams

For Facebook, generous limits make discipline more important. Local events, fundraisers and group updates should put date, place, price and action in the opening sentence. Long background paragraphs belong after the facts. If a reader needs to decide whether to attend, buy, donate or reply, those details should not be hidden.

For YouTube, title length carries search, recommendation and human scanning duties at once. Keep the main search phrase within the first 55 to 60 characters where possible. Use the description for context, links and credits. A 100-character title is allowed, but a shorter title is often easier to read on mobile.

For bios, count every punctuation mark and line break before pasting. A good bio states who the account serves, what it publishes and where to go next. Remove decorative separators first. They consume space without adding meaning and often look messy when a platform changes line wrapping.

  • Draft the first 140 characters separately for channels with previews.
  • Keep a short version and a full version for approval workflows.
  • Treat hashtags, mentions and link text as part of the visible message.
  • Check counts again after adding tracking links or campaign codes.

A working review process

A useful review starts with the job of the post. If the job is awareness, the first sentence needs the news. If the job is response, the first sentence needs the action. If the job is conversion, the first sentence needs the offer or outcome. Character counting keeps that discipline visible because every weak opener has a measurable cost.

Paste the approved draft into the counter, record the full count, then test the opener alone. If the opener cannot stand without the remaining text, rewrite it. This small step prevents the common failure where all the useful information sits after a truncation point.

Finally, test the copy in the publishing product. Third-party counters cannot account for every media attachment, mention, paid format or platform experiment. The counter is the editorial checkpoint; the platform composer is the final acceptance test.

Examples from a campaign review

Imagine a webinar announcement going to X, LinkedIn and Instagram. The raw copy says: "We are pleased to announce that our September webinar about reducing checkout abandonment is now open for registration, with practical examples from recent ecommerce audits." That is a reasonable sentence, but it tries to be the same sentence everywhere.

For X, the useful version is tighter: "Checkout abandonment webinar: 3 audit fixes that recovered lost revenue. Register for September." That leaves room for a link and keeps the topic clear. For LinkedIn, the same idea can carry a short problem statement before the link because the audience expects more context. For Instagram, the caption should connect to the visual and move the registration link instruction near the start.

This is why one master draft is not enough. Count each channel version separately and keep the strongest noun in the preview. The count tells you where to cut, but the channel tells you what to keep.

What to record for repeat work

For recurring publishing, keep a small log of final counts. Record the channel, the full character count, the opener count, whether the post used hashtags, and whether the post included a link. After ten or twenty posts, patterns become visible. You may find that event posts need more room than product updates, or that customer-story posts work better when the first sentence is under 120 characters.

This record is not a performance dashboard. It is an editing memory. It stops the team relearning the same limit every week and makes onboarding faster for new writers. It also gives stakeholders a calm answer when they ask why a favourite phrase was cut: the phrase was not wrong, it was too late in the visible preview.
