The Complete Guide to Character Limits on Major Platforms

Published 1 September 2026. Updated 1 September 2026. 7 min read.

Character limits sit in different places: some are hard technical limits, some are display limits, and some are editorial targets that prevent truncation. Treating them as one thing causes mistakes. A 160-character SMS is a billing boundary. A 155-character meta description is a display target. A 3,000-character LinkedIn post is a maximum, not a recommendation.

Hard limits versus useful limits

A hard limit rejects copy when you exceed it. SMS segments, app name fields and many profile fields behave this way. A useful limit is softer: the platform accepts longer text, but readers do not see the important part. Email subjects, SEO descriptions and social previews often sit in this second group.

For hard limits, count before the final paste. For useful limits, count the preview line separately. If a LinkedIn post is 2,000 characters but the first 180 characters are weak, the full count is not the issue. The opening budget is.

Teams should record both numbers in briefs. "Meta description: 154 characters" is useful. "Opening sentence: 92 characters" is often more useful because it tells reviewers what a reader sees first.

Reference limits to keep close

Plan X posts around 280 characters for standard public copy. Plan LinkedIn posts around a 3,000-character upper boundary, while making the first two lines carry the point. Plan Instagram and TikTok captions around 2,200 characters, with hashtags included in the count.

Use 100 characters as a YouTube title ceiling and write important words early. Use roughly 150 to 160 characters for meta descriptions and 50 to 60 for title tags. For email subjects, 30 to 50 characters is a practical working range because mobile inboxes often show less than desktop clients.

For SMS, 160 GSM characters is the classic single-message segment. Some characters and emoji can move a message into Unicode encoding, where the single-message segment is shorter. Marketing teams should test real messages in their sending platform before approval.

How to use the cheat sheet

The character limits cheat sheet gives the quick number. This guide explains how to use the number. Start with the destination: social post, search snippet, app listing, SMS, subject line or interface field. Then decide whether the limit is technical, visual or editorial.

Next, draft the copy without watching every character. A full first draft preserves the idea. Then paste it into the counter and make targeted reductions. Cut weak openings first, then duplicated nouns, then soft adjectives. Keep facts, prices, dates and product names until the last pass.

Finally, test the exact final text where it will publish. Counters are editorial tools. They do not know whether a platform counts URLs differently, whether a paid ad format has a stricter rule, or whether a CMS appends a brand suffix.

Build limits into reviews

A good approval note includes the copy, the count and the target. For example: "Email subject, 42 characters, target under 50". That line prevents vague feedback such as "make it punchier" and gives the reviewer a constraint to work with.

For high-volume teams, create snippets for common limits in tickets and briefs. "Meta title, target 50 to 60", "SMS body, target one segment", "App name, hard maximum 30". Repetition turns character counting into a habit rather than a late-stage fix.

A practical planning table

For each campaign or page, create five columns: destination, field, target count, hard maximum and final count. A social launch might include X post, LinkedIn opener, Instagram caption, email subject, SMS reminder and meta description. Seeing those fields together stops teams treating one long master line as if it belongs everywhere.

The table also exposes where variants are needed. A phrase that works in a 3,000-character LinkedIn post may be unusable in a 50-character subject line. Rather than trimming the same wording until it breaks, write a fresh version for the smaller field. Short fields need a sharper job, not a shrunken paragraph.

Keep the final counts with the approved copy. When the same campaign returns next quarter, the previous counts give the new writer a starting point and prevent avoidable debate.

When exact limits change

Platform limits change, paid formats differ from organic formats, and some accounts receive features before others. That is why this site separates working references from final acceptance. The cheat sheet is a practical starting point. The publishing platform is the authority for the final paste.

When you discover a changed limit, update the team brief and link to the source where possible. Do not rely on memory. A small change, such as a store title field moving from one limit to another, can affect localisation, screenshots, campaign copy and release notes.

For public pages, add a visible "Last updated" date. It shows readers that the reference is maintained and gives editors a reason to check older guidance before using it in a live review.

How to brief a writer

A useful brief gives the writer the field, audience, action and target count. "Write a 45-character subject for lapsed subscribers" is far clearer than "write a short email line". Add examples of words that must stay, such as a product name, date, discount code or legal phrase.

When the writer returns the copy, ask for the count beside each option. This keeps feedback specific and makes it easier to choose between clarity, tone and space.
